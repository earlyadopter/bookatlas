# 4. The Early Republic and the First Party System (1791–1824)

The framers distrusted political parties, yet parties appeared almost immediately, born of a fundamental argument about how the new nation should develop. The young republic navigated financial crises, foreign wars, and a doubling of its territory, all while inventing the practice of self-government. This chapter follows the first party system and the challenges of the early republic.

## 4.1 Hamilton, Jefferson, and the first parties

Almost as soon as Washington's government began, his cabinet split. Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton envisioned a commercial, industrial nation with a strong central government, a national bank, and close ties to Britain. Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson favored an agrarian republic of independent farmers, limited federal power, and sympathy for revolutionary France.

Around these visions formed the first two parties. The Federalists, led by Hamilton and John Adams, championed national power and order. The Democratic-Republicans (Jeffersonian Republicans), led by Jefferson and Madison, championed states' rights and strict reading of the Constitution. Their rivalry defined the first party system—and established that organized parties, whatever the framers hoped, were here to stay.

## 4.2 Washington's precedents

As the first president, George Washington knew his every act set a precedent. He established the cabinet, kept the nation neutral in the wars of the French Revolution, and firmly but restrainedly put down the Whiskey Rebellion (1794), demonstrating that federal law had teeth.

Most consequentially, he stepped down after two terms, setting an informal limit that held for 150 years. His Farewell Address (1796) warned against permanent foreign alliances and, pointedly, against the "baneful effects of the spirit of party." The warning went unheeded—the parties were already forming—but his voluntary surrender of power remains among his most important legacies.

## 4.3 The Adams years and a peaceful transfer

John Adams, a Federalist, won the presidency in 1796. His term was dominated by conflict with France and by bitter partisanship at home. In 1798 the Federalist Congress passed the Alien and Sedition Acts, which restricted immigrants and criminalized harsh criticism of the government—measures Republicans denounced as tyranny.

Jefferson and Madison responded with the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions, arguing that states could challenge unconstitutional federal laws. The election of 1800 was ferocious, but when Jefferson won, power passed peacefully from one party to its rival for the first time. This "Revolution of 1800" proved that a republic could change rulers by ballots, not bloodshed.

## 4.4 Jefferson in power

Once in office, Jefferson governed more pragmatically than his rhetoric suggested. He cut taxes and shrank the government, yet in 1803 he seized the chance to buy the Louisiana Territory from France, doubling the nation's size—a bold use of federal power that strained his own strict-constructionist principles.

He dispatched the Lewis and Clark expedition to explore the vast new lands. But foreign troubles mounted as Britain and France, at war, seized American ships and sailors. Jefferson's trade embargo, meant to pressure both powers, mainly harmed American merchants. His successor, Madison, would inherit a drift toward war.

## 4.5 The judiciary asserts itself

The Constitution created a Supreme Court but left its power vague. Under Chief Justice John Marshall, appointed by Adams in 1801, the Court became a coequal branch. In Marbury v. Madison (1803), Marshall established judicial review—the Court's authority to strike down laws that violate the Constitution.

Over three decades, Marshall's Court consistently strengthened national power over the states, upholding the national bank in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) and federal control of interstate commerce. Though Marshall was a Federalist and his party faded, his interpretations endured, embedding a strong national judiciary into the constitutional system long after the Federalists themselves disappeared.

## 4.6 The War of 1812 and the end of the first party system

Frustrated by British interference and encouraged by "War Hawks" hungry for expansion, Madison led the nation into the War of 1812. The war was militarily inconclusive—the British burned Washington, but Americans held at Baltimore and won a famous victory at New Orleans after peace was already signed.

The war's political effect was decisive at home. The Federalists, who had opposed it and flirted with disunion at the Hartford Convention, were discredited and soon collapsed. The 1810s became the "Era of Good Feelings," with the Republicans virtually unopposed. But one-party calm proved unstable: new divisions were already forming beneath the surface.

## 4.7 Meanwhile, elsewhere (1791–1824)

These decades were dominated by the French Revolution and Napoleon, whose wars engulfed Europe until his final defeat at Waterloo (1815). The victorious powers redrew the map at the Congress of Vienna, seeking to restore order and suppress revolution. The Industrial Revolution, meanwhile, was transforming Britain and beginning to spread.

Across Latin America, the age of revolution bore fruit: leaders such as Simón Bolívar and José de San Martín led wars of independence that freed most of Spanish America by the 1820s. In 1823, the United States responded with the Monroe Doctrine, warning European powers against further colonization in the hemisphere—an early claim to a role beyond its borders.
