# 2. The Growth of the Presidency

The office of president has expanded far beyond anything the framers imagined. They designed a limited executive, checked by Congress and the courts, and expected the legislature to be the dominant branch. Over two centuries, war, crisis, and the ambitions of individual presidents transformed the office into the most powerful position in the world. This essay traces that growth.

## 2.1 A deliberately limited office

The framers, having just rebelled against a king, were wary of executive power. They gave the president real but bounded authority: to command the armed forces, conduct diplomacy, veto legislation, and execute the laws Congress passed. Congress, they assumed, would be the heart of the government, holding the powers of taxing, spending, and declaring war.

They also built in checks: impeachment, Senate confirmation of appointments and treaties, and regular elections. The presidency was meant to provide "energy in the executive"—decisive leadership—without becoming a monarchy. For much of the nineteenth century, the office remained modest, and Congress usually led. The vast modern presidency would have astonished its creators.

## 2.2 Washington sets the mold

As the first president, George Washington shaped the office by example. Aware that his every act set a precedent, he governed with deliberate restraint. He established the cabinet, asserted control over foreign policy, and demonstrated federal authority by suppressing the Whiskey Rebellion—but always within careful limits.

His most important decision was to leave. By stepping down after two terms, Washington established a norm of voluntary limits on power that endured for a century and a half. He modeled a presidency of dignity and restraint, powerful enough to lead but humble enough to relinquish office. This early self-discipline gave the young republic confidence that executive power need not become tyranny.

## 2.3 Jackson and the popular presidency

Andrew Jackson transformed the presidency into a popular office. Claiming to represent the whole people—unlike Congress, elected in mere districts and states—he wielded the veto aggressively, defied the Supreme Court, and dominated his era so thoroughly that critics dubbed him "King Andrew."

Jackson's innovation was to root presidential power in a direct connection to the voters. He argued that the president alone was chosen by the entire nation and thus embodied the popular will. This claim, controversial in his day, became a permanent feature of the office. Every later president who has appealed over the head of Congress directly to the people has followed the path that Jackson first blazed.

## 2.4 Lincoln and war powers

The Civil War vastly expanded presidential power. Facing the gravest crisis in the nation's history, Abraham Lincoln took extraordinary actions, often without waiting for Congress: he expanded the army, blockaded Southern ports, suspended the writ of habeas corpus, and issued the Emancipation Proclamation as a war measure.

Lincoln justified these steps as necessary to preserve the Union under his duty to "take care that the laws be faithfully executed" and his role as commander in chief. Critics charged him with overreach; defenders saw a president meeting an existential threat. Either way, the war showed how national emergencies could stretch the office far beyond its peacetime bounds—a lesson later presidents would remember and invoke.

## 2.5 The modern presidency emerges

The twentieth century made the presidency the center of national government. Theodore Roosevelt treated the office as a "bully pulpit," using it to shape public opinion and drive reform. Franklin Roosevelt then permanently enlarged it: his New Deal made the president responsible for the economy, and his four terms and wartime leadership concentrated unprecedented authority in the White House.

An enormous federal bureaucracy grew under presidential direction, and the president became the chief legislator, proposing the agenda Congress debated. After FDR, the Twenty-Second Amendment (1951) limited presidents to two terms—a reaction to his long tenure. But the modern, activist presidency he created, dominating both domestic and foreign policy, was here to stay.

## 2.6 The "imperial presidency" and its checks

The Cold War further swelled presidential power, especially over foreign and military affairs. Presidents committed forces to major wars in Korea and Vietnam without formal declarations, and controlled nuclear weapons and vast intelligence agencies. Critics warned of an "imperial presidency" escaping meaningful constraint.

The backlash came in the 1970s. The Vietnam War and the Watergate scandal, which forced Richard Nixon to resign in 1974, prompted Congress to reassert itself through measures like the War Powers Resolution. Yet the fundamental expansion endured. The tug-of-war between an assertive executive and a Congress seeking to rein it in continues today, a defining and unresolved tension of the modern constitutional system.

## 2.7 The presidency today

The modern president stands at the center of American life in a way the framers never envisioned: chief executive, commander in chief, head of a global superpower, party leader, and the single figure most Americans hold responsible for the state of the nation. Television, and later social media, made the president a constant national presence.

This concentration of attention and expectation brings enormous power but also profound risk. Presidents can act swiftly in crisis but may also strain the system of checks the framers built. The recurring debate—how much power should one person hold in a republic?—remains as alive now as when Washington first weighed every precedent. The presidency's growth is, in miniature, the story of the changing American state.
