# 3. The Constitution's Amendments as a Story

The Constitution has been amended only twenty-seven times in more than two centuries, and each amendment tells a story about what Americans came to believe their founding document lacked. Read in sequence, the amendments trace an expanding definition of liberty and citizenship—though the path was neither straight nor smooth. This essay follows that story from the Bill of Rights to the Twenty-Seventh Amendment.

## 3.1 The Bill of Rights

The original Constitution contained few explicit protections for individual liberties, an omission that nearly sank its ratification. Anti-Federalists demanded guarantees against government overreach, and Federalists promised to add them. In 1791, the first ten amendments—the Bill of Rights—were ratified together.

They enumerate now-famous freedoms: speech, press, religion, and assembly (First Amendment); the right to bear arms (Second); protections against unreasonable searches (Fourth); guarantees of fair trials and due process (Fifth through Eighth); and reservations of power to the people and the states (Ninth and Tenth). The Bill of Rights answered a founding fear—that a distant government would trample individual liberty—and remains the most frequently invoked part of the Constitution.

## 3.2 The Reconstruction Amendments

After the Civil War, three amendments remade the Constitution to address slavery and citizenship. The Thirteenth (1865) abolished slavery throughout the nation. The Fourteenth (1868) granted citizenship to all born in the country and required states to provide "equal protection of the laws" and "due process." The Fifteenth (1870) barred denying the vote on the basis of race.

Together these are sometimes called a "second founding," extending the Constitution's promises to millions of formerly enslaved people. Yet their promise was betrayed for a century, as Southern states used terror and legal trickery to nullify them. Much later, the Fourteenth Amendment in particular would become the foundation for the civil rights revolution and countless expansions of individual rights.

## 3.3 The Progressive Era amendments

The early twentieth century produced a burst of amendments reflecting Progressive reform. The Sixteenth (1913) authorized a federal income tax, giving the government a powerful new revenue source. The Seventeenth (1913) provided for the direct election of senators, who had previously been chosen by state legislatures, making the Senate more democratic.

The Eighteenth (1919) imposed national Prohibition of alcohol, the great reform crusade of the era. The Nineteenth (1920) prohibited denying the vote on the basis of sex, enfranchising millions of women after a decades-long struggle. These amendments show the Progressive faith that the Constitution could be actively used to democratize government and reshape society—for better, and, in Prohibition's case, in ways soon regretted.

## 3.4 Second thoughts and fine-tuning

Not every amendment expanded rights; some corrected earlier mistakes or adjusted the machinery of government. The Twenty-First Amendment (1933) repealed the Eighteenth, ending Prohibition after it bred crime and proved unenforceable—the only time an amendment has undone another.

Others refined how government works. The Twentieth (1933) moved up the dates when new presidents and Congresses take office. The Twenty-Second (1951) limited presidents to two terms, a reaction to Franklin Roosevelt's four elections. The Twenty-Fifth (1967) clarified presidential succession and disability. These "housekeeping" amendments reveal a Constitution being maintained and repaired, its practical workings adjusted as experience exposed gaps and ambiguities.

## 3.5 Expanding the vote

A recurring theme of the amendments is the steady widening of the right to vote. The Fifteenth and Nineteenth extended it across race and sex. In the modern era, three more amendments continued the expansion. The Twenty-Third (1961) gave residents of the District of Columbia votes in presidential elections.

The Twenty-Fourth (1964) abolished the poll tax in federal elections, removing a device long used to keep poor and Black citizens from voting. The Twenty-Sixth (1971) lowered the voting age to eighteen, driven partly by the argument that those old enough to be drafted for war in Vietnam were old enough to vote. Together, these amendments tell a story of democracy gradually, if unevenly, opening its doors.

## 3.6 The strange tale of the Twenty-Seventh

The most recent amendment has the oddest history. The Twenty-Seventh Amendment, which bars any change in congressional pay from taking effect until after the next election, was originally proposed in 1789 as part of the package that became the Bill of Rights—but it fell short of ratification and was largely forgotten.

Two centuries later, a college student named Gregory Watson discovered that the amendment had no deadline and could still be ratified. He launched a campaign to revive it, and states gradually approved it until it was finally ratified in 1992—203 years after it was proposed. Its story is a reminder that the Constitution is a living document with a long memory, and that even a single determined citizen can change it.

## 3.7 What the amendments reveal

Taken together, the amendments trace the evolution of American ideals. The broad arc runs toward greater liberty and inclusion: the Bill of Rights guarding individuals against government, the Reconstruction Amendments extending citizenship, and a long series of measures expanding the vote to ever more Americans.

Yet the story also shows how hard change is. Amending the Constitution requires supermajorities in Congress and among the states, so only the most broadly supported changes succeed—and thousands of proposed amendments have failed. This difficulty makes the document stable but slow to change, forcing most constitutional evolution to come through interpretation by the courts. The twenty-seven amendments are the rare moments when Americans agreed, formally and overwhelmingly, to rewrite their fundamental law.
