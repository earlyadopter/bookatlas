# 6. Slavery, Realignment, and the Republican Party (1854–1861)

In just seven years, the second party system shattered and a new one took its place, organized squarely around slavery. The Whig Party collapsed, a wholly new Republican Party rose to challenge the spread of slavery, and the nation slid toward disunion. This chapter follows the realignment that produced the parties we still know today—and the crisis that broke the Union apart.

## 6.1 The act that broke the parties

In 1854, Senator Stephen Douglas pushed through the Kansas-Nebraska Act, which allowed settlers in those territories to decide for themselves whether to permit slavery. This repealed the Missouri Compromise line that had kept slavery out of northern territories for a generation, and it enraged the North.

The act detonated the party system. The Whigs, split hopelessly between North and South, disintegrated. In the ensuing chaos, anti-slavery Whigs, disaffected Democrats, and members of the small Free Soil movement began forming a new organization dedicated to stopping slavery's expansion. Within two years it had a name—the Republican Party—and startling momentum across the North.

## 6.2 The new Republican Party

The Republican Party of the 1850s was a Northern, free-labor party. It did not, at first, call for abolishing slavery where it already existed, but it firmly opposed extending slavery into the western territories, which it wanted reserved for free farmers and workers.

Its coalition blended former Whigs, anti-slavery Democrats, immigrants, and reformers under the banner of "free soil, free labor, free men." The party championed a dynamic, opportunity-driven vision of the North against what it called the "Slave Power"—the political dominance of Southern slaveholders. In 1856, its first presidential run fell short but showed astonishing strength. A genuinely new party had arrived, and the old order would not return.

## 6.3 Bleeding Kansas and mounting violence

Kansas became a battleground. Pro-slavery and anti-slavery settlers poured in, each determined to control the territory's future, and rival governments claimed authority. Fraud, intimidation, and armed clashes gave the territory a grim nickname: "Bleeding Kansas."

The violence reached Congress itself. In 1856, a South Carolina congressman beat Massachusetts Senator Charles Sumner unconscious with a cane on the Senate floor after Sumner denounced slavery—an act cheered in much of the South and condemned in the North. The caning showed how far the sections had drifted apart. Compromise, the habit that had held the Union together, was breaking down into raw hostility.

## 6.4 Dred Scott and a hardening divide

In 1857 the Supreme Court tried to settle the slavery question by force of law and instead inflamed it. In Dred Scott v. Sandford, Chief Justice Roger Taney ruled that Black Americans were not citizens and could not sue, and that Congress had no power to ban slavery in any territory.

The decision thrilled the South and horrified the North, seeming to make slavery a national institution beyond democratic control. It also energized the Republicans, whose entire platform the Court had just declared unconstitutional. In 1858, a little-known Illinois Republican named Abraham Lincoln argued in famous debates that the nation could not endure "half slave and half free."

## 6.5 1860: a nation splits at the ballot box

The election of 1860 fractured along sectional lines. The Democratic Party split in two, running separate Northern and Southern candidates, while a fourth party sought the middle. The Republicans nominated Abraham Lincoln on a platform of halting slavery's expansion.

> A house divided against itself cannot stand.

Lincoln won the presidency with almost no Southern support, carrying the populous North. To many white Southerners, his victory meant the federal government had passed permanently into hostile, anti-slavery hands. The normal machinery of democracy had produced a result that a large section of the country refused to accept. Secession followed within weeks.

## 6.6 Secession

Beginning with South Carolina in December 1860, seven Deep South states seceded before Lincoln even took office, declaring their independence to protect slavery, which their own declarations named as the central cause. In early 1861 they formed the Confederate States of America.

Lincoln denied that any state could lawfully leave the Union and refused to recognize secession, but he pledged not to attack. The crisis came at Fort Sumter, a federal fort in Charleston harbor. When Lincoln moved to resupply it, Confederate forces opened fire in April 1861. Four more states joined the Confederacy. The long argument over slavery had finally become war.

## 6.7 Meanwhile, elsewhere (1854–1861)

As the United States fractured, other nations were being built. In Europe, movements to unify Italy and Germany gathered force, soon to create powerful new states through war and diplomacy. Britain and France fought Russia in the Crimean War (1853–1856), a bloody conflict that exposed the horrors of modern combat.

Elsewhere, empires strained. In 1857 a massive rebellion against British rule in India led Britain to abolish the East India Company and impose direct crown control. Russia moved toward emancipating its serfs in 1861, the same year America went to war partly over its own system of bondage. Around the world, the mid-century was an age of consolidation, empire, and reckoning with unfree labor.
