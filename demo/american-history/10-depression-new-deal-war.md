# 10. Depression, New Deal, and World War (1929–1945)

The stock market crash of 1929 plunged the nation into the Great Depression, the worst economic catastrophe in its history. In response, Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal redefined the role of government and forged a political coalition that would dominate for decades. Then a second world war remade both the nation and its place in the world.

## 10.1 The Great Depression

After the 1929 crash, the economy spiraled downward. Banks failed by the thousands, businesses collapsed, and unemployment soared to roughly one in four workers. Families lost homes and farms; shantytowns spread; a devastating drought turned the southern plains into a "Dust Bowl," driving hundreds of thousands from their land.

President Herbert Hoover, a Republican, believed recovery should come mainly from private initiative and limited government action, and he resisted large-scale federal relief. As suffering deepened and his response seemed inadequate, public anger grew. The Depression discredited the small-government, pro-business creed that had dominated the 1920s and set the stage for a dramatic political turn.

## 10.2 The New Deal

Franklin D. Roosevelt won the presidency in 1932 promising a "New Deal" and radiating confidence.

> The only thing we have to fear is fear itself.

His administration launched an unprecedented flood of programs to provide relief, recovery, and reform: banking regulation, public works and jobs programs, aid to farmers, and protections for workers' right to organize. The Social Security Act (1935) created old-age pensions and unemployment insurance, building the foundation of the American welfare state.

The New Deal did not fully end the Depression—that took the war—but it eased suffering and reshaped expectations. Government would now be held responsible for the health of the economy and the security of citizens, a profound and lasting change.

## 10.3 The New Deal coalition

Roosevelt's response transformed the parties. He assembled a broad and durable alliance—the New Deal coalition—that made the Democrats the nation's majority party for a generation. It united Northern urban workers, immigrants and their children, union members, farmers, white Southerners, and, in a historic shift, most Black voters, who moved from the "party of Lincoln" to the Democrats.

The Democratic Party now stood for active government, economic security, and support for labor and the "common man." Republicans became the party of business and limited government, warning that the New Deal threatened liberty and fiscal soundness. This alignment—Democrats as the party of government activism—would frame American politics into the 1960s and beyond, even as its internal tensions grew.

## 10.4 The coalition's contradictions

The New Deal coalition held together an unstable mix. Northern Black voters and white Southern segregationists belonged to the same party, an alliance that required Roosevelt to soft-pedal civil rights to keep powerful Southern Democrats in Congress on his side. Many New Deal programs were administered in ways that excluded or shortchanged Black Americans.

This contradiction—a party of both racial liberals and staunch segregationists—was papered over as long as economic issues dominated. But it could not last forever. When civil rights forced its way onto the national agenda in the decades after the war, the strains within the Democratic coalition would eventually tear its regional foundations apart.

## 10.5 The road to war

Through the 1930s, most Americans wanted no part of the world's mounting crises, and Congress passed Neutrality Acts to keep the nation out of foreign wars. But aggression spread: Japan invaded China, and Nazi Germany, under Adolf Hitler, conquered much of Europe by 1940.

Roosevelt gradually moved to aid the Allies, notably through Lend-Lease, which supplied Britain and later the Soviet Union. Isolationists resisted fiercely. The debate ended abruptly on December 7, 1941, when Japan attacked the U.S. naval base at Pearl Harbor. The United States declared war, entering a global conflict that would demand total national mobilization.

## 10.6 World War II

The United States mobilized on a staggering scale, its factories becoming the "arsenal of democracy" and its armed forces swelling to millions. Americans fought across two vast theaters, in Europe against Germany and Italy and across the Pacific against Japan, in alliance with Britain and the Soviet Union.

The war reshaped society at home: women entered factories in huge numbers, Black Americans pressed for a "Double V"—victory abroad and against discrimination at home—and Japanese Americans were unjustly interned. Allied victory came in 1945, in Europe in May and over Japan in August after atomic bombs devastated Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The war ended the Depression and left the United States the world's dominant power.

## 10.7 Meanwhile, elsewhere (1929–1945)

The Depression was global, and its misery helped fascism and militarism rise in Germany, Italy, and Japan, as desperate peoples turned to authoritarian leaders promising order and glory. The result was World War II, the deadliest conflict in human history, which killed an estimated 60 million or more people worldwide.

At its center lay the Holocaust, the Nazi genocide of six million Jews and millions of others. The war shattered the old European order, exhausted Britain and France, and left two superpowers standing: the United States and the Soviet Union. Their wartime alliance, always uneasy, would swiftly give way to a new global rivalry—the Cold War.
