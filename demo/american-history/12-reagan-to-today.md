# 12. From Reagan to Today (1980–present)

The last four decades saw a conservative revolution, the sudden end of the Cold War, the rise of the digital age, and the hardening of a deeply polarized political landscape. The realignment set in motion by the civil rights era matured into the red-and-blue map familiar today. This chapter brings the story to the present.

## 12.1 The Reagan revolution

Ronald Reagan's election in 1980 marked a decisive conservative turn. He argued that "government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem," and he set out to cut taxes, reduce regulation, and shrink domestic spending while sharply increasing military budgets. His approach, dubbed "supply-side economics," reshaped economic debate.

Reagan's presidency redefined the Republican Party as the champion of small government, low taxes, a strong defense, and traditional social values, uniting economic conservatives with a growing religious right. His personal optimism and political skill made conservatism the dominant force in American politics, shifting the center of debate rightward and forcing Democrats to adapt over the following decades.

## 12.2 The Cold War ends

Reagan pursued the Cold War aggressively, expanding the military and challenging the Soviet Union, even while later negotiating major arms reductions with Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev. As the 1980s closed, the communist system, strained by economic failure and reform, collapsed with astonishing speed.

In 1989 the Berlin Wall fell, and by 1991 the Soviet Union itself dissolved. The Cold War that had defined global politics for over four decades was suddenly over, leaving the United States as the world's sole superpower. Americans greeted the moment with triumph and optimism, expecting a peaceful new era of expanding democracy and free markets—a hope that would prove more complicated than it first appeared.

## 12.3 The 1990s: a new center

In the 1990s Democrat Bill Clinton adapted his party to the post-Reagan landscape, embracing free trade, welfare reform, and fiscal discipline while defending core social programs—a "New Democrat" approach that accepted parts of the conservative turn. The decade saw strong economic growth, budget surpluses, and the dawn of the internet age.

Yet partisanship intensified. Clinton faced fierce opposition from a resurgent conservative Congress under Newt Gingrich and was impeached by the House in 1998, though acquitted by the Senate. The era's prosperity coexisted with deepening cultural and political divisions. Beneath the calm surface of the "end of history," the forces of polarization that would define the next era were already gathering strength.

## 12.4 September 11 and its wars

On September 11, 2001, terrorists linked to al-Qaeda hijacked airliners and destroyed New York's World Trade Center and struck the Pentagon, killing nearly 3,000 people. The attacks stunned the nation and reoriented American foreign policy around a "war on terror."

President George W. Bush launched wars in Afghanistan (2001) and Iraq (2003), the latter deeply controversial and based partly on flawed intelligence about weapons that were never found. The conflicts proved long and costly, entangling the United States in the Middle East for years. At home, the government expanded surveillance and security powers, sparking lasting debates over the balance between liberty and safety.

## 12.5 Obama, Trump, and a divided nation

In 2008, amid a severe financial crisis, Barack Obama was elected the first Black president, a milestone many saw as a landmark in the nation's long struggle with race. He responded to the Great Recession and passed a major health care law, but faced fierce opposition and rising partisan gridlock.

In 2016, Donald Trump won the presidency as a populist outsider, channeling the grievances of many voters against political and cultural elites and reshaping the Republican Party around economic nationalism and hard-line immigration policy. His tenure further polarized the country. The disputed aftermath of the 2020 election, culminating in the January 6, 2021 attack on the Capitol, revealed how bitterly divided the nation had become over its basic political processes.

## 12.6 The parties today

The realignment born in the 1960s has fully matured. Today's Democratic Party draws its strength from cities, younger and more diverse voters, the coasts, and the college-educated, championing an active government role in health care, climate, and economic fairness. The Republican Party dominates rural areas, the South, and among many working-class white voters, emphasizing lower taxes, restricted immigration, gun rights, and traditional values.

The regional bases have completely reversed from a century ago: the once-Democratic "Solid South" is now reliably Republican, while the once-Republican Northeast leans Democratic. "Liberal" and "conservative" now map onto the two parties far more cleanly than in the past. The result is intense polarization, with Americans increasingly divided not just over policy but over facts, identity, and the nature of the country itself.

## 12.7 Meanwhile, elsewhere (1980–present)

The end of the Cold War unleashed a wave of globalization, as trade, technology, and communication knit the world ever closer. The internet and smartphones transformed daily life everywhere. China, embracing market reforms while keeping one-party rule, rose to become an economic superpower and America's chief rival.

New challenges crossed all borders: terrorism, the 2008 global financial crisis, climate change, mass migration, and the COVID-19 pandemic that began in 2020. Democracy, triumphant in 1991, faced renewed challenges from authoritarian powers and populist movements worldwide. The United States entered its third century still the world's leading power, but in a more crowded, contested, and interconnected world than ever before.
