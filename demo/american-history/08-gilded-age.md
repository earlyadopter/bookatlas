# 8. Industry, Immigration, and the Gilded Age (1877–1900)

In the decades after Reconstruction, the United States became an industrial giant. Railroads spanned the continent, factories and cities boomed, and millions of immigrants arrived. Enormous fortunes rose beside grinding poverty, giving the era its ironic name, the "Gilded Age," coined by Mark Twain to suggest a glittering surface over deep problems.

## 8.1 An industrial colossus

American industry exploded after the Civil War. Abundant resources, a growing labor force, new technologies, and a national railroad network combined to make the United States the world's leading industrial economy by 1900. Steel, oil, and railroads formed the backbone of this transformation.

Towering entrepreneurs dominated whole industries: Andrew Carnegie in steel, John D. Rockefeller in oil, financiers like J. P. Morgan in banking. Admirers called them "captains of industry" for building the modern economy; critics called them "robber barons" for crushing competition and exploiting workers. Their vast trusts and monopolies concentrated wealth and power to a degree that alarmed many Americans and provoked demands for reform.

## 8.2 A nation of immigrants and cities

Industry drew people to the cities in unprecedented numbers. Millions of immigrants arrived, increasingly from southern and eastern Europe—Italians, Poles, Jews, and others—alongside Chinese laborers in the West until exclusion laws barred them. Cities such as New York and Chicago swelled with newcomers.

Urban life offered opportunity but also overcrowded tenements, disease, and dangerous work. Political "machines" like New York's Tammany Hall traded services for immigrant votes, blending genuine help with corruption. The mixing of peoples reshaped American culture even as it stirred nativist backlash. The country was becoming more diverse, more urban, and more unequal all at once.

## 8.3 Labor fights back

Industrial workers faced long hours, low pay, and hazardous conditions, and they organized to resist. Labor unions such as the Knights of Labor and later the American Federation of Labor pressed for shorter hours and better wages. The struggle was often violent.

Major upheavals punctuated the era: the Great Railroad Strike of 1877, the Haymarket affair of 1886, the Homestead strike of 1892, and the Pullman strike of 1894. Employers, backed by courts and troops, frequently broke strikes by force. Though the labor movement won few immediate victories, it planted the idea that workers had collective rights—an idea that would grow powerful in the next century.

## 8.4 Party politics of the Gilded Age

Gilded Age politics was fiercely competitive but often short on substance. Republicans and Democrats were closely matched nationally, and elections turned out huge numbers of voters. Yet the parties differed less over grand principle than over patronage, tariffs, and regional loyalty.

The Republicans remained the party of the North, business, high protective tariffs, and the legacy of the Union. The Democrats held the "Solid South" and drew urban immigrant and working-class support in the North. Corruption was rampant in both, and reformers pushed for a professional civil service to replace the spoils system, achieving the Pendleton Act (1883). Real ideological realignment, however, still lay ahead.

## 8.5 The Populist revolt

While industrialists prospered, farmers struggled with falling crop prices, heavy debts, and the power of railroads and banks. Their anger fueled a mass movement. In 1892, they formed the People's Party, or Populists, demanding radical reforms: government control of railroads, a graduated income tax, and an expanded money supply to ease debts.

The Populists channeled deep grievances against concentrated economic power. In 1896, their cause merged with the Democratic campaign of William Jennings Bryan, who thundered against the gold standard. Bryan lost, and the Populist Party faded, but its ideas did not die. Many would be taken up by the reformers of the coming Progressive Era.

## 8.6 The nadir of race relations

For Black Americans, especially in the South, these decades marked a brutal retreat from the promise of Reconstruction. Southern states imposed "Jim Crow" laws mandating racial segregation and used poll taxes, literacy tests, and outright terror to strip Black citizens of the vote guaranteed by the Fifteenth Amendment.

In Plessy v. Ferguson (1896), the Supreme Court blessed segregation under the doctrine of "separate but equal," giving legal cover to systematic inequality. Lynching claimed thousands of Black lives, terrorizing communities into submission. Leaders debated how to respond, from Booker T. Washington's emphasis on economic self-help to demands for full civil and political rights. The constitutional amendments endured, but their meaning had been hollowed out.

## 8.7 Meanwhile, elsewhere (1877–1900)

Europe's powers raced to carve up Africa in the "Scramble" of the 1880s and 1890s, dividing nearly the entire continent among themselves at conferences where no African was present. Imperial rivalry stretched across Asia and the Pacific as well. Britain stood at the height of its global empire.

Industrialization spread to Germany, which challenged Britain economically, and to Japan, now a rising Asian power. The era's confident faith in progress, science, and empire masked mounting tensions among the great powers. As the century closed, the United States joined the imperial contest itself, defeating Spain in 1898 and acquiring overseas territories—a prelude to its emergence as a world power.
