# 9. Progressive Era and World Power (1900–1929)

As the new century opened, Americans set out to tame the excesses of the Gilded Age through reform, while the nation stepped decisively onto the world stage. The Progressive movement reshaped both parties, a global war drew the country into international leadership, and the 1920s roared with prosperity, cultural change, and new anxieties.

## 9.1 The Progressive impulse

Progressivism was a broad reform spirit rather than a single program. Its adherents believed that expert knowledge, active government, and an engaged citizenry could cure the ills of industrial society—corruption, monopoly, poverty, and unsafe conditions. Journalists called "muckrakers" exposed scandals from corporate abuses to filthy meatpacking plants.

Reformers won concrete changes: regulation of food and drugs, limits on child labor, and measures to make government more democratic, such as the direct election of senators (Seventeenth Amendment) and, in many states, the initiative and referendum. Progressivism cut across party lines, animating reformers among both Republicans and Democrats, and it permanently expanded expectations of what government should do.

## 9.2 Roosevelt, Taft, and Wilson

Three presidents defined the era. Republican Theodore Roosevelt, taking office in 1901, made the presidency a "bully pulpit," busting trusts, protecting consumers, and conserving wilderness. His successor, William Howard Taft, continued trust-busting but split with Roosevelt, who bolted in 1912 to run on the Progressive "Bull Moose" ticket.

The Republican split handed victory to Democrat Woodrow Wilson, who pursued his own reform agenda: lower tariffs, a new income tax, the Federal Reserve, and stronger antitrust laws. That both parties produced major reformers shows how thoroughly progressivism reshaped politics. Yet Wilson, a Southerner, also imposed racial segregation on the federal workforce—a reminder of progressivism's blind spots.

## 9.3 Votes for women

The long campaign for women's suffrage reached its climax in these years. Building on the movement launched at Seneca Falls decades earlier, a new generation of activists organized marches, lobbying, and civil disobedience. Western states led the way, granting women the vote before the nation did.

Women's contributions during World War I strengthened the argument that they deserved full citizenship. In 1920, the Nineteenth Amendment prohibited denying the vote on the basis of sex, enfranchising millions of women nationwide. It was the largest single expansion of democracy in American history to that point—though in practice, many women of color, especially in the South, remained blocked from voting.

## 9.4 Becoming a world power

The United States entered the century as a rising power with a new overseas empire won from Spain, including the Philippines and Puerto Rico. Roosevelt projected American might, building a modern navy and securing the Panama Canal (completed 1914), which linked the oceans and confirmed U.S. dominance in the hemisphere.

For decades Americans had heeded Washington's warning against foreign entanglements. But growing economic and naval power made isolation harder to maintain. The nation increasingly intervened in Latin America and looked outward across the Pacific. When world war engulfed Europe, the United States would find neutrality impossible to sustain—and would emerge transformed.

## 9.5 The Great War

When World War I erupted in Europe in 1914, the United States tried to stay neutral. But German submarine warfare, which sank ships carrying Americans, and other provocations pushed opinion toward war. In 1917 the country entered the conflict on the side of Britain and France.

Fresh American troops and resources helped tip the balance toward Allied victory in 1918. President Wilson then championed an idealistic peace, including a League of Nations to prevent future wars, set out in his Fourteen Points.

> The world must be made safe for democracy.

But the Senate, wary of foreign commitments, rejected the League. America retreated toward isolation, even as it had become a decisive global force.

## 9.6 The Roaring Twenties

The 1920s brought prosperity, mass consumption, and cultural upheaval. Automobiles, radio, and movies knit the nation together and created a modern consumer culture. Cities pulsed with energy; the Harlem Renaissance flowered as Black artists and writers reshaped American culture, and jazz became the era's soundtrack.

Beneath the glitter ran deep tensions. Prohibition banned alcohol but bred crime and hypocrisy. Nativism surged, producing harsh immigration quotas and a revived Ku Klux Klan. A "Red Scare" targeted radicals and immigrants. Republicans dominated national politics with a pro-business, small-government creed, presiding over a boom that seemed endless. In October 1929, the stock market crash abruptly ended the party and ushered in catastrophe.

## 9.7 Meanwhile, elsewhere (1900–1929)

World War I devastated Europe, killing millions and toppling four empires—German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman, and Russian. In 1917, revolution brought the Bolsheviks to power in Russia, creating the world's first communist state, the Soviet Union, whose ideology would challenge the capitalist order for the rest of the century.

The postwar settlement redrew Europe's map and imposed harsh terms on Germany, sowing resentments that would fuel a second, greater war. Elsewhere, anti-colonial movements stirred in India and beyond, and Japan continued its rise. The 1920s' fragile prosperity was global, and so, soon, would be its collapse.
