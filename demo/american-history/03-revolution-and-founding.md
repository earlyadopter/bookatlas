# 3. Revolution and Founding (1763–1791)

In a single generation, thirteen British colonies became an independent republic with a written constitution. The break was neither inevitable nor unanimous, and the new nation's founding documents contained both soaring ideals and unresolved compromises. This chapter traces the road from imperial crisis to the ratified Constitution and Bill of Rights.

## 3.1 The road to rebellion

Victory in the Seven Years' War left Britain deep in debt and eager to make the colonies help pay for their own defense. Parliament imposed new taxes—the Stamp Act (1765), the Townshend duties, and later a tax on tea. Colonists, accustomed to taxing themselves through their assemblies, objected fiercely to "taxation without representation."

Protest escalated from petitions and boycotts to violence, including the Boston Massacre (1770) and the Boston Tea Party (1773). Britain answered with the Coercive Acts, closing Boston's port and tightening control. In 1774 the colonies convened a Continental Congress to coordinate resistance. What began as a dispute over taxes was becoming a debate over the very nature of authority.

## 3.2 Independence declared

Fighting broke out at Lexington and Concord in April 1775. For a year, many colonists still hoped for reconciliation, but Thomas Paine's pamphlet Common Sense (1776) made a blistering popular case for independence. On July 4, 1776, the Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence, drafted chiefly by Thomas Jefferson.

> We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal.

Those words announced a revolutionary principle even as the society proclaiming it kept millions enslaved and denied women political rights. The Declaration's ideals would be invoked by generations of reformers who insisted the nation live up to them.

## 3.3 Winning the war

The War of Independence lasted eight years. The Continental Army under George Washington rarely won decisive victories but survived, wearing down British resolve. The turning point came at Saratoga (1777), whose American victory persuaded France to enter the war as an ally, bringing crucial money, troops, and naval power.

The decisive blow fell at Yorktown (1781), where a combined American and French force trapped a British army. The Treaty of Paris (1783) recognized American independence and generous borders stretching to the Mississippi. Independence was won, but the fragile confederation of states now faced the harder task of governing itself.

## 3.4 The Articles and their failure

The first national government, under the Articles of Confederation (ratified 1781), deliberately kept power weak. Fearful of a strong central authority like the one they had just overthrown, the states created a Congress that could not tax, regulate trade, or enforce its decisions.

The result was mounting trouble: unpaid debts, trade disputes among states, and no way to respond to unrest such as Shays's Rebellion (1786–1787), an uprising of indebted Massachusetts farmers. Many leaders concluded that the union would collapse without a stronger framework. In 1787, delegates gathered in Philadelphia, ostensibly to revise the Articles—and instead wrote an entirely new constitution.

## 3.5 Writing the Constitution

The Constitutional Convention crafted a stronger national government balanced by checks among three branches. The Great Compromise settled a fierce dispute between large and small states, creating a House apportioned by population and a Senate with equal state representation.

Slavery shadowed the proceedings. The notorious Three-Fifths Compromise counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for representation, boosting Southern power, and the Constitution shielded the slave trade until 1808. These bargains held the union together at a terrible moral cost. The framers designed a system meant to be durable yet amendable—flexible enough, they hoped, to survive disputes they could not themselves resolve.

## 3.6 Ratification and the first divide

The proposed Constitution had to be ratified by the states, and the debate split Americans into two camps. Federalists, including Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay—authors of The Federalist Papers—argued that a strong union was essential to survival. Anti-Federalists feared a distant government would crush liberty and demanded protections for individual rights.

This argument produced the Constitution's first great compromise after ratification: the promise of a Bill of Rights, added in 1791 as the first ten amendments. It also foreshadowed the party divisions to come. The very question of how much power the national government should hold would organize American politics for the next two centuries.

## 3.7 Meanwhile, elsewhere (1763–1791)

The American Revolution was part of an age of revolutions. Its success, and its ideals of natural rights and popular sovereignty, inspired reformers across the Atlantic. In 1789, France erupted in a far more radical revolution that toppled its monarchy and shook every European throne.

Elsewhere, Britain, having lost its American colonies, turned its imperial energies toward India and the Pacific. Spanish and Portuguese America remained under colonial rule, but revolutionary ideas were spreading there too. The world was entering a turbulent era in which the question America had just posed—by what right does any government rule?—would be asked again and again.
