# 7. Civil War and Reconstruction (1861–1877)

The Civil War was the deadliest conflict in American history and the great turning point of the nation's story. It destroyed slavery, settled the question of secession, and vastly strengthened the national government. The uncertain peace that followed, Reconstruction, briefly promised a genuine multiracial democracy before that promise was violently abandoned.

## 7.1 A war of unprecedented scale

The Confederacy sought only to defend its independence; the Union had to conquer a vast territory to restore itself. The North held major advantages in population, industry, railroads, and naval power, while the South counted on defensive fighting, cotton diplomacy, and superior early leadership.

The war ground on for four years and cost roughly 750,000 lives. Battles such as Antietam and Gettysburg (1863) killed thousands in a day. Industrial-scale warfare—rifled muskets, railroads, ironclads, and total mobilization—made this a grimly modern conflict. What many expected to be a short war became a prolonged struggle that reshaped both the nation and the meaning of the Union cause.

## 7.2 Emancipation

At first Lincoln framed the war narrowly, as a fight to preserve the Union, not to end slavery. But as the conflict deepened, emancipation became both a moral aim and a military strategy. The Emancipation Proclamation (January 1, 1863) declared enslaved people in Confederate-held areas to be free.

The proclamation transformed the war into a struggle for freedom and cleared the way for Black men to serve in the Union Army; nearly 200,000 did. Permanent abolition came with the Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in 1865, which ended slavery everywhere in the United States. The nation's founding contradiction had finally been confronted in blood.

## 7.3 Union victory and Lincoln's death

By 1864, Union armies under Ulysses S. Grant and William T. Sherman waged relentless campaigns that broke Southern resistance. Sherman's march through Georgia devastated the Confederacy's capacity to fight. In April 1865, General Robert E. Lee surrendered to Grant at Appomattox, effectively ending the war.

Days later, Lincoln was assassinated by a Confederate sympathizer—the first American president to be murdered. He had already sketched a generous vision of reunion. His death removed the leader best positioned to guide reconstruction and left the enormous task of rebuilding, and defining freedom for four million former slaves, to less capable hands.

## 7.4 The promise of Reconstruction

Reconstruction sought to rebuild the South and define the place of freed people in American life. The Republican-controlled Congress, clashing bitterly with President Andrew Johnson, drove an ambitious program. Two landmark amendments enshrined new rights: the Fourteenth (1868) granted citizenship and equal protection to all born in the country, and the Fifteenth (1870) barred denying the vote based on race.

For a time, the promise seemed real. Black men voted in large numbers and were elected to state legislatures and Congress. Freed people built churches, schools, and communities. Backed by federal troops and the Freedmen's Bureau, a genuine experiment in multiracial democracy took root across the South.

## 7.5 The parties after the war

The war remade the parties. The Republicans were now the party of the Union, emancipation, and a strong, activist federal government, drawing overwhelming support in the North and, briefly, among newly enfranchised Black Southerners. They also championed business, tariffs, and national economic development.

The Democrats survived as the party of white Southerners and many Northern working-class and immigrant voters, defending states' rights and, in the South, white supremacy. This alignment—"the party of Lincoln" versus a Democratic "Solid South"—would define American politics for generations. Its eventual reversal, a century later, is one of the great stories of the nation's political history.

## 7.6 The abandonment of Reconstruction

Reconstruction's promise was crushed by violence and fatigue. White supremacist groups such as the Ku Klux Klan terrorized Black voters and their allies, while Northern commitment waned amid economic depression and scandal. One by one, Southern states fell back under the control of white "Redeemer" Democrats.

The disputed election of 1876 brought the reckoning. In the Compromise of 1877, Republicans kept the presidency in exchange for withdrawing the last federal troops from the South. With federal protection gone, Southern states moved to strip Black citizens of their rights. The constitutional amendments remained on the books, but their promise would lie unfulfilled for nearly a century.

## 7.7 Meanwhile, elsewhere (1861–1877)

The 1860s and 1870s were a great age of nation-building abroad. Italy completed its unification, and in 1871 the German states united into a powerful new empire after defeating France. Japan's Meiji Restoration (1868) launched a stunning drive to modernize and industrialize, transforming a feudal society into a rising power within a generation.

Elsewhere, European empires pressed deeper into Africa and Asia, and the Suez Canal (1869) reshaped global trade. Russia's emancipation of its serfs (1861) paralleled America's abolition of slavery. Around the world, strong central states were consolidating power—much as the American Civil War had transformed a loose union into a more unified nation.
