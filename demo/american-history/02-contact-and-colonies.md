# 2. Contact and Colonies (1492–1763)

The meeting of two worlds after 1492 reshaped both. European empires planted colonies along the Atlantic seaboard, Native nations were devastated by disease yet remained powerful players, and the forced migration of enslaved Africans became central to the colonial economy. Out of this violent, creative mixing emerged the societies that would one day rebel.

## 2.1 The Columbian Exchange

Columbus's voyages opened a two-way transfer of people, plants, animals, and microbes now called the Columbian Exchange. From the Americas came maize, potatoes, tomatoes, and tobacco, which transformed diets worldwide. From Europe came horses, cattle, pigs, wheat, and sugar cane.

The deadliest travelers were invisible. Smallpox, measles, and influenza, to which Native peoples had no immunity, swept through communities with catastrophic force. In many regions populations fell by 70 to 90 percent within a century or two. This demographic collapse, more than any battle, cleared the way for European settlement and reshaped Native societies that survived.

## 2.2 Rival empires

Spain built the first and largest American empire, conquering the Aztec and Inca states and extracting silver that flowed across the globe. France established a fur-trading network along the St. Lawrence and Mississippi, generally seeking alliances with Native nations rather than large settlements. The Dutch planted New Netherland on the Hudson, a commercial hub of striking diversity.

England came later but settled densely. Its colonies drew families, not just soldiers and traders, and grew a large European population along the coast. These empires competed for land, trade, and souls, and their rivalries repeatedly drew Native nations and, eventually, colonists into war.

## 2.3 Thirteen different colonies

The English colonies were never a single society. New England, settled heavily by Puritans, built town-centered communities around religion, farming, and shipping. The Middle Colonies—New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey—were diverse in religion and ethnicity, with Pennsylvania's Quakers promoting relative tolerance.

The Southern colonies developed plantation economies growing tobacco, rice, and indigo for export, and became increasingly dependent on enslaved labor. These regional differences—in religion, economy, and the scale of slavery—would echo through American history for centuries, shaping conflicts that long outlasted the colonial era.

## 2.4 Slavery takes root

Enslaved Africans were brought to the Americas beginning in the 1500s, and to England's mainland colonies from 1619 onward. Over time, colonial laws hardened slavery into a hereditary, racial institution: enslaved status passed from mother to child and was justified by emerging ideas of racial hierarchy.

By the mid-1700s, slavery existed in every colony but was concentrated in the South, where it underpinned the plantation economy. The transatlantic slave trade forced millions of Africans across the ocean in brutal conditions. This system of bondage created enormous wealth for some and became, from the start, a profound contradiction at the heart of a society that would soon proclaim liberty.

## 2.5 Early self-government

English colonists brought traditions of representative government and expanded them. Virginia's House of Burgesses (1619) and the Mayflower Compact (1620) were early experiments in self-rule. Most colonies developed elected assemblies that controlled taxation and local affairs, while governors represented the Crown.

Property-owning white men could often vote—a broader franchise than in Europe, though still narrow. Colonists came to see these assemblies as their local parliaments, protectors of their "rights as Englishmen." This habit of self-government, practiced for generations, made later interference from London feel not merely costly but illegitimate.

## 2.6 Meanwhile, elsewhere (1492–1763)

While the colonies grew, Europe was convulsed by religion and dynastic war, from the Reformation to the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648), which ended with a new order of sovereign states. Scientific revolution and, by the 1700s, Enlightenment thought advanced ideas of reason, natural rights, and government by consent that would echo in America.

Elsewhere, the Ottoman, Mughal, and Qing empires ruled vast populations and stood among the world's great powers. The Atlantic slave trade drew Africa, Europe, and the Americas into a single, brutal commercial system. By 1763, Britain had defeated France in a global conflict (the Seven Years' War), gaining North American dominance—and the debts and dilemmas that would soon spark revolution.
