# 5. Jacksonian Democracy and the Second Party System (1824–1854)

The one-party calm of the Era of Good Feelings gave way to a vibrant, sometimes rowdy new democracy. Voting expanded to nearly all white men, mass political parties organized the electorate, and Andrew Jackson became the era's dominant and divisive figure. This chapter follows the rise of the second party system: Democrats versus Whigs.

## 5.1 The disputed election and a new coalition

In 1824 four candidates split the vote, and the House chose John Quincy Adams over Andrew Jackson, who had won the most votes. Jackson's furious supporters cried "corrupt bargain" and organized for revenge. Out of this grievance emerged a new party built on mass appeal: the Democratic Party.

Led by Jackson and the organizational genius Martin Van Buren, the Democrats celebrated the common man, states' rights, and limited federal government. They swept Jackson into the presidency in 1828 on a wave of expanded voting rights. American politics had entered a new age of parties, conventions, rallies, and unabashed popular campaigning.

## 5.2 Jackson and the Democrats

Jackson cast himself as champion of the ordinary white citizen against entrenched elites. His Democrats stood for a small federal government, hostility to special privilege, hard money, and the spread of white settlement westward. He rewarded loyal supporters with government jobs—the "spoils system"—and wielded the veto more aggressively than any predecessor.

His defining fight was against the Second Bank of the United States, which he saw as a corrupt tool of the wealthy. He vetoed its recharter and destroyed it, triggering financial instability. To admirers, Jackson embodied democratic energy; to critics, he was "King Andrew," a demagogue trampling the balance of powers.

## 5.3 The Whigs emerge

Jackson's forceful presidency provoked an opposition that coalesced by the mid-1830s into the Whig Party. Led by Henry Clay and Daniel Webster, the Whigs took their name from British opponents of royal power, casting Jackson as a would-be king.

Where Democrats distrusted federal activism, the Whigs embraced Clay's "American System": a national bank, protective tariffs, and federally funded roads and canals to knit the economy together. They tended to draw support from commercial and manufacturing interests, reform-minded Protestants, and those who saw government as an engine of national improvement. For two decades, Democrats and Whigs competed fiercely and nationally, each with real strength in every region.

## 5.4 Removal and the Trail of Tears

Jacksonian democracy's promise did not extend to Native Americans. Under Jackson, Congress passed the Indian Removal Act (1830), authorizing the forced relocation of southeastern nations to lands west of the Mississippi to open their territory for white settlement and cotton.

The Cherokee fought removal in court and won a favorable Supreme Court ruling, which Jackson's administration simply ignored. In 1838–1839, the U.S. Army drove thousands of Cherokee westward on the brutal Trail of Tears, on which thousands died of cold, hunger, and disease. Removal exposed the harsh limits of a democracy defined by race, expanding rights for white men while dispossessing others.

## 5.5 Reform and reawakening

The same democratic ferment fueled a wave of reform movements. A religious revival, the Second Great Awakening, convinced many Americans that they could perfect society. Reformers campaigned for temperance, public schools, humane prisons and asylums, and women's rights.

At the Seneca Falls Convention (1848), activists including Elizabeth Cady Stanton issued a Declaration of Sentiments demanding equality for women, including the vote. Above all, a growing abolitionist movement, led by figures such as William Lloyd Garrison and Frederick Douglass, condemned slavery as a sin. These moral crusades increasingly strained a party system that had tried to keep slavery out of national politics.

## 5.6 Expansion and its explosive question

The 1840s were gripped by "Manifest Destiny," the belief that the United States was destined to span the continent. The annexation of Texas and victory in the Mexican-American War (1846–1848) brought vast new territories, including California, into the Union.

But every new territory reopened an explosive question: would it be slave or free? The Compromise of 1850 patched over the crisis temporarily, admitting California as free while strengthening the Fugitive Slave Act. The compromise satisfied no one for long. Expansion had turned slavery from a regional institution into the central, unavoidable issue of national politics.

## 5.7 Meanwhile, elsewhere (1824–1854)

Europe seethed with the tension between old monarchies and new demands for liberty and nationhood. In 1848, revolutions swept across the continent—France, Germany, Italy, and Austria—most crushed within a year, though they planted ideas that endured. Britain, avoiding revolution, industrialized rapidly and expanded its global empire.

Elsewhere, China was shaken by the First Opium War (1839–1842), which forced open its ports to British trade and began a century of foreign encroachment. In the newly independent states of Latin America, the promise of liberation gave way to instability and strongman rule. Around the world, the questions of the age were nation, liberty, and who counted as a citizen.
