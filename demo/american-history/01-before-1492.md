# 1. Before 1492 — the peoples of the continent

Long before European ships reached its shores, North America was home to hundreds of distinct nations with their own languages, economies, religions, and forms of government. The story of American history does not begin in 1776, or even in 1492; it begins with the peopling of a continent over many thousands of years. This chapter sketches that deep past and the world beyond it.

## 1.1 A continent already peopled

Human beings reached the Americas at least 15,000 years ago, and quite possibly earlier, migrating from Asia during the last Ice Age when lower sea levels exposed a land bridge across what is now the Bering Strait. Some groups may also have moved south along the Pacific coast by boat. Over millennia, their descendants spread across two continents and adapted to nearly every environment on Earth—Arctic tundra, eastern woodlands, desert Southwest, Great Plains, and Pacific coast.

By 1492 the Americas held tens of millions of people. Estimates vary widely, but the hemisphere was densely inhabited, not the "empty wilderness" of later myth. These were not a single people but a vast mosaic of societies, as different from one another as the nations of Europe, speaking languages belonging to dozens of unrelated families.

## 1.2 Cities, farms, and trade

Native North Americans built sophisticated societies. Around Cahokia, near present-day St. Louis, a Mississippian city of perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 people flourished around 1100 CE, centered on enormous earthen mounds. In the Southwest, Ancestral Puebloan peoples raised stone and adobe complexes at Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde, linked by roads and aligned to the sky.

Agriculture reshaped the continent. The "three sisters"—maize, beans, and squash—spread north from Mexico and supported growing populations. Trade networks moved copper from the Great Lakes, shells from the Gulf, and obsidian across immense distances. These were connected worlds, with their own centers, peripheries, and long histories of rise and decline.

## 1.3 Ways of governing

There was no single Native form of government, but many. Some peoples lived in small, mobile bands led by consensus; others built powerful confederacies. The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy of the Northeast bound together five, later six, nations under a Great Law of Peace, coordinating war and diplomacy while preserving each nation's autonomy.

Authority often rested on persuasion, kinship, and reciprocity rather than coercion. Leaders were expected to give generously and speak well; many decisions required broad agreement. Women held significant power in matrilineal societies, controlling land and choosing or removing chiefs. These traditions of deliberation and balance later drew the admiration—and selective borrowing—of European observers.

## 1.4 Many worlds, many lifeways

The continent held a staggering variety of ways of life shaped by land and climate. On the Great Plains, peoples hunted bison on foot for centuries before the horse—reintroduced by Europeans—transformed their societies. In the Pacific Northwest, abundant salmon supported wealthy, settled communities famous for totem art and elaborate ceremonial feasts.

Across the Southwest, desert farmers built irrigation canals; in the eastern woodlands, peoples combined farming with hunting and fishing. Spiritual life was equally varied, though many peoples shared a sense of the natural world as alive with power and of humans as part of, not above, creation. This diversity is essential to remember: there was no single "Indian" way of life, but hundreds of distinct cultures, each with its own history, language, and identity.

## 1.5 Meanwhile, elsewhere (to 1492)

While these societies matured in North America, the wider world was busy and interconnected. In the 1400s, China's Ming dynasty sent Admiral Zheng He on vast treasure voyages across the Indian Ocean before turning inward. West Africa's Mali and Songhai empires grew rich on gold and salt; Timbuktu was a renowned center of learning.

In Europe, the Renaissance was reviving classical learning, and the 1450s printing press began spreading ideas faster than ever. The Ottoman Empire captured Constantinople in 1453, prompting Europeans to seek new sea routes to Asian markets. Portugal was already probing the African coast. When Columbus sailed west in 1492, he was not discovering an unknown planet but connecting worlds that had long developed apart—a collision that would transform all of them.
